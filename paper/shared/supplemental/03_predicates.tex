\section{Diagnostic Predicate Derivations}

The diagnostic checker converts simulated state traces into named predicate terms. This section
states the predicates in a form that can be inspected without reading the implementation. The
thresholds are owned by configuration and dated records; the equations below explain how recorded
state variables enter the gate.

\subsection{Drop/Settle Predicate}

Let $x_t=(q_t,\dot q_t)$ denote the body state at time $t$, $z_t$ the relevant support or body
height, $v_t$ the linear speed, $z_f$ the floor height, and $T$ the final simulation step. The
drop/settle probe starts with a positive height margin and asks whether the package descends,
avoids floor breach, and settles:
\[
  A_{\mathrm{drop}} =
  C_{\mathrm{descent}} \land C_{\mathrm{height}} \land C_{\mathrm{speed}}.
\]
The three clauses are
\begin{align}
  C_{\mathrm{descent}} &= [z_0 - z_T \geq \epsilon_{\mathrm{drop}}],\\
  C_{\mathrm{height}} &= [z_T \geq z_f - \epsilon_z],\\
  C_{\mathrm{speed}} &= [\|v_T\|_2 \leq \epsilon_v].
\end{align}
The implementation may emit multiple labels for one failure because the clauses are not mutually
exclusive. A package can fail by not descending, by breaching the floor, by retaining residual
speed, or by combining these symptoms. This is why the main paper reports named labels rather than
only a binary fail cell.

\caption{Supplement-only drop/settle predicate anatomy. The visual separates initial height,

\paragraph{What this shows.}
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-predicate-drop-settle} shows how one vertical probe becomes an interpretable
set of terms. The final label is not hand-written prose; it follows from terminal speed, terminal
height, and descent checks under recorded settings.

\paragraph{What this does not show.}
The drop/settle predicate is not a general proof of physical correctness. It is a diagnostic task
with specific solver settings, initial state, and thresholds.

\subsection{Stack-or-Slide Predicate}

The stack-or-slide scene treats the candidate package as a support surface and places a small probe
object above it. Let $c_T$ be terminal contact, $r_{xy}=\|\pi_{xy}(x_T-x_0)\|_2$ be horizontal
drift, $h_T$ be final probe support height, and $v_T$ be final probe speed. A compact gate is
\[
  A_{\mathrm{stack}} =
  [c_T=1] \land [r_{xy}\leq \epsilon_{xy}]
  \land [h_T \geq h_{\min}] \land [\|v_T\|_2 \leq \epsilon_v].
\]
This predicate separates three visually similar cases. The probe may touch but slide away; it may
remain near the support but fall through; or it may stay on the support while retaining too much
motion. Each symptom points to a different debugging action for the package.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/generated/supplement/supplement_predicate_stack_slide.pdf}
\caption{Supplement-only stack-or-slide predicate anatomy. Terminal contact is necessary but not
sufficient; drift, support height, and residual speed are checked separately.}
\label{fig:supp-predicate-stack-slide}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What this shows.}
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-predicate-stack-slide} teaches why a package can appear to support the probe
in one frame while still failing the diagnostic. The measured drift and residual speed remain part
of the gate.

\paragraph{What this does not show.}
The predicate is not a manipulation-stability benchmark and does not imply that a robot can use
the object in contact-rich operation.

\subsection{Sphere-Rain Predicate}

The sphere-rain probe uses a deterministic set of lightweight contact probes. Let $R$ be the probe
set and $H(r,P_g)$ indicate whether probe $r$ generated an acceptable contact observation against
package $P_g$. A useful summary is
\[
  \rho(P_g) = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{r\in R} H(r,P_g).
\]
The checker can then compare $\rho$ with a configured interval and emit labels for missing contact
coverage or excessive contact counts. The purpose is not exhaustive coverage of all possible
contacts. The purpose is to expose obvious holes, blocked openings, or contact inflation under a
fixed and reproducible probe cloud.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/generated/supplement/supplement_predicate_sphere_rain.pdf}
\caption{Supplement-only sphere-rain contact-coverage teaching plate. The probe cloud, contact
bins, and emitted label are shown as separate audit objects.}
\label{fig:supp-predicate-sphere-rain}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What this shows.}
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-predicate-sphere-rain} shows how repeated small probes become a coverage
diagnostic. This helps explain why a package can pass a simple contact canary yet still deserve a
more specific warning.

\paragraph{What this does not show.}
The figure does not claim exhaustive geometric coverage, calibrated contact fidelity, or broad
benchmark superiority over convex-decomposition baselines.
